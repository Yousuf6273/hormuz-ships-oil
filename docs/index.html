Can ships predict oil prices?

I counted every tanker crossing the Strait of Hormuz each day for nearly five years and tested whether fewer crossings warn of a higher Brent oil price. In normal times they don't. In the March 2026 crisis, the oil market moved first.

What I found

The hypotheses and tests were written down and committed before any data was downloaded.

  • In normal times (2022 to mid-2025), ships don't predict oil, and oil doesn't predict ships. A 10% drop in tanker crossings was followed by Brent moving about −0.1% (the wrong direction, not significant). On data it hadn't seen (July 2025 onwards), the model did worse than simply assuming the average.
  • In the March 2026 crisis, both reacted to the same news, and oil moved first. Brent rose about 8% more than stocks explain in the two weeks before ships stopped. Ships stopped over the weekend of 28 Feb to 1 Mar; Brent jumped +7% at Monday's close, its first chance to react.
  • After the closure, oil kept moving (+57% relative to stocks by day 20) while crossings stayed near zero. The ship count had nothing new to say.
  • Cargo and fishing boats (the placebo) show no signal either. The pre-registered hypothesis is not supported.
  • Extensions: after the closure, trade moved to Fujairah, outside the strait, while Dubai's Jebel Ali stayed at about a quarter of normal. Satellite radar confirms the traffic collapse was real, though some ships kept moving without AIS.

How it works

  1. Count crossings. Two boxes either side of 56.5°E. A ship seen in one box and then the other within 24 hours is one crossing (at most one per ship every 6 hours). Tankers are Global Fishing Watch's tanker class.
  2. No peeking at the future. Each trading day only uses ships counted up to the end of the previous day.
  3. Test both directions. Regressions with stock-market and VIX controls, lead–lag correlations, Granger tests both ways, and an event study for March 2026.
  4. Keep score honestly. A held-out test period (from July 2025), a placebo (cargo and fishing), and corrections for running many tests.
